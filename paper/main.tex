\begin{abstract}
Foundation models for protein and nucleotide sequences are increasingly used for zero-shot variant effect prediction, yet systematic comparisons across modalities are lacking. We benchmark 12 protein and nucleotide foundation models on 29,595 ClinVar missense variants under a strict gene-disjoint evaluation protocol, and validate on a temporally independent dataset (9,627 variants, Jan 2025). We find that protein and nucleotide models encode fundamentally different signals: their pairwise score correlations are near zero ($r \approx 0.00$), confirmed by CKA (mean cross-modality CKA = 0.031). This orthogonality is not a failure mode -- it reflects that protein models capture amino-acid-level biophysics while nucleotide models encode regulatory context absent from missense variants. Even EVO2-7B, the largest nucleotide model (7B parameters), achieves AUROC 0.836 on missense variants --- impressive for a DNA-only model but still below ESM1b (0.882), confirming that protein-level information provides an irreducible advantage for amino-acid substitutions. We further evaluate two evolution-aware methods with precomputed scores: GPN-MSA (AUROC 0.898) and EVE/popEVE (0.888), both of which outperform individual foundation models by leveraging population-scale evolutionary conservation. We demonstrate protein--nucleotide complementarity in two orthogonal directions. First, on ProteinGym deep mutational scanning assays, protein models achieve Spearman $\rho = 0.22$--$0.32$ while nucleotide models perform near chance ($\rho \leq 0.12$). Second, on ClinVar splice-region variants, nucleotide models capture modest signal (AUROC 0.640) while protein models are at chance (0.500) by construction, since intronic splice variants leave the protein sequence unchanged. We show that a lightweight meta-learner fusing protein scores with physicochemical features achieves AUROC 0.933 under gene-disjoint evaluation, approaching AlphaMissense (0.948) without population frequency data. We further introduce \textbf{Cross-Modal Attention Fusion (CMAF)}, a novel architecture that learns per-variant attention weights to adaptively combine protein (ESM1b) and DNA (EVO2) signals. CMAF achieves AUROC 0.928 on D1 and 0.902 on D2, with the gating module learning to weight DNA features 11$\times$ more than protein features (0.92 vs 0.08), revealing that EVO2 provides complementary evolutionary conservation signal that complements ESM1b's amino acid-level effects. Our analysis also reveals two methodological pitfalls: training-serving skew in linear probing (AUROC inflation of 0.02--0.07 due to label leakage through shared protein contexts) and gamma parameter fragility in RBF-CKA (bandwidth $\gamma=1$ vs median heuristic produces qualitatively different CKA values). We provide corrected analysis scripts and recommend reporting both CKA parameterizations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The prediction of whether a genetic variant is pathogenic or benign is central to clinical genomics \cite{rentzsch2019predicting}. Two families of foundation models now offer zero-shot variant effect prediction: protein language models \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans, li2023esm1b, lin2023evolutionary, esm3team2024esm3, meier2021language} trained on amino-acid sequences, and nucleotide models \cite{dalla2024nucleotide, zeng2024dnabert2, nguyen2023hyenadna, singh2024genalm} trained on genomic DNA. In clinical variant interpretation, practitioners face a practical dilemma: which model family should be trusted for a given variant? The choice is currently made ad hoc, because no prior work characterizes what each modality encodes, whether their signals are redundant or complementary, and under which biological conditions each excels or fails.

Existing benchmarks leave this question unanswered. They evaluate protein and nucleotide models in isolation on missense variants \cite{promponas2020predicting, pei2021identifying}, using random train/test splits that leak gene-level information and inflate performance. No prior work (1)~compares protein and nucleotide models on the same variants under identical conditions, (2)~tests whether their score disagreement reflects complementary information or noise, or (3)~quantifies the practical benefit of fusing both modalities.

We address all three gaps. We benchmark 7 protein and 5 nucleotide foundation models on 29,595 ClinVar missense variants using a strict \textbf{gene-disjoint} evaluation protocol (zero gene overlap between train and test), validated on a temporally independent dataset (9,627 variants). We then test the complementarity hypothesis on two external benchmarks where the expected signal differs by modality: ProteinGym deep mutational scanning (where protein models should capture amino-acid biophysics) and splice-region variants (where nucleotide models should capture regulatory context invisible to protein sequences).

We find that protein and nucleotide models encode \textbf{orthogonal} signals: their pairwise score correlations are near zero ($r \approx 0.00$, mean cross-modality CKA = 0.031), and each modality excels exactly where its inductive bias predicts. Even EVO2-7B, the largest nucleotide model (7B parameters), achieves AUROC 0.836 on missense variants --- impressive for a DNA-only model, but still below ESM1b (0.882), confirming that protein-level information provides an irreducible advantage for amino-acid substitutions. This orthogonality is not a failure but a design consequence: protein models capture biophysical constraints on amino-acid substitutions (Spearman $\rho = 0.22$--$0.32$ on ProteinGym DMS), while nucleotide models capture splice-disruption signal (AUROC 0.640) that protein models cannot access. We show that a lightweight meta-learner fusing protein scores with physicochemical features achieves AUROC 0.933 under gene-disjoint evaluation, approaching AlphaMissense (0.948) without population frequency data. We also identify and correct two methodological pitfalls: training-serving skew in linear probing and gamma parameter fragility in RBF-CKA.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Controlled cross-modal benchmark.} First systematic comparison of protein and nucleotide foundation models on the same variants under identical conditions, using gene-disjoint evaluation (29,595 ClinVar missense variants; 9,627 temporally independent validation). Includes EVO2-7B, the largest nucleotide model (7B), enabling direct comparison with protein models.
    \item \textbf{Complementarity through orthogonal encoding.} We demonstrate that protein and nucleotide models encode non-redundant signals: protein models capture amino-acid biophysics ($\rho = 0.22$--$0.32$ on ProteinGym DMS, AUROC 0.882 on missense), while nucleotide models capture splice-disruption context (AUROC 0.640 on splice variants) where protein models are at chance by construction. This orthogonality is confirmed by CKA (mean cross-modality = 0.031) and pairwise score correlations ($r \approx 0.00$).
    \item \textbf{Practical fusion.} A lightweight meta-learner fusing protein scores with physicochemical features achieves AUROC 0.933 under gene-disjoint evaluation, approaching AlphaMissense (0.948) without population frequency data. We further propose \textbf{Cross-Modal Attention Fusion (CMAF)}, a novel architecture with cross-modal attention and gating that achieves AUROC 0.928 (D1) and 0.902 (D2), learning to adaptively weight protein vs DNA modalities per variant.
    \item \textbf{Methodological corrections.} Two pitfalls identified and corrected: (1)~training-serving skew in linear probing inflates AUROC by 0.02--0.07 through gene-level label leakage, and (2)~RBF-CKA bandwidth $\gamma$ fragility produces qualitatively different similarity values; we recommend the median heuristic and report both parameterizations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{D1 (Primary):} 29,595 missense variants (15,540 Pathogenic + 14,055 Benign) across 6,371 genes from ClinVar.

\textbf{D2 (Temporal Holdout):} 9,627 missense variants (3,865 Pathogenic + 5,762 Benign) from a later ClinVar release (Jan 2025), spanning 2,569 genes. All D2 genes are a subset of D1 genes.

Both datasets use protein contexts (wild-type/mutant sequences, length 512) and nucleotide contexts (10,000 bp windows centered on the variant). We additionally evaluate on quality-filtered subsets defined by ClinVar star ratings: Star 2+ (12,131 variants, multiple independent submitters) and Star 3+ (1,420 variants, expert panel review).

\subsection{Gene-Disjoint Evaluation}

We use \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} with \texttt{gene\_symbol} as the group key ($test\_size = 0.2$, $random\_state = 42$): 5,096 train genes (23,113 variants), 1,275 test genes (6,482 variants), \textbf{zero gene overlap}. We also report random-split results for comparison.

\subsection{Models}

We benchmark 12 foundation models: \textbf{Protein:} ESM-1b (650M), ESM-2 (3B), ESM-1v (650M), ESM3 (1.4B), ProtBERT (350M), ProtT5 (3B), Ankh (500M). \textbf{Nucleotide:} EVO2-7B (7B), Nucleotide Transformer v2 (500M), DNABERT-2 (350M), GenaLM (370M), HyenaDNA (1.6B). \textbf{Evolution-aware baselines:} GPN-MSA \cite{benegas2025gpn} (precomputed genome-wide scores from 100-way vertebrate alignment), EVE/popEVE \cite{frazer2021eve} (variational autoencoder on protein families). \textbf{External baselines:} AlphaMissense \cite{cheng2023alphamissense} and REVEL \cite{ioannidis2016revel}.

\subsection{Scoring Paradigms}

We employ two scoring paradigms for zero-shot variant effect prediction.

\textbf{MLM Score:} For protein models with masked language model heads, we compute the log-likelihood ratio:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{MLM}} = \log P(x_{\text{mut}} \mid x_{\text{context}}) - \log P(x_{\text{wt}} \mid x_{\text{context}})
    \label{eq:mlm}
\end{equation}
where $x_{\text{mut}}$ and $x_{\text{wt}}$ are the mutant and wild-type amino acids, and $x_{\text{context}}$ is the surrounding sequence. More negative values indicate more deleterious substitutions.

\textbf{Cosine Similarity:} For encoder-only models (ProtT5, Ankh), we compute embedding similarity:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{cos}} = \frac{\mathbf{h}_{\text{mut}} \cdot \mathbf{h}_{\text{wt}}}{\|\mathbf{h}_{\text{mut}}\| \|\mathbf{h}_{\text{wt}}\|}
    \label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_{\text{mut}}$ and $\mathbf{h}_{\text{wt}}$ are mean-pooled embeddings. Lower values indicate more disruptive substitutions.

\textbf{EVO2 Scoring:} For EVO2, we compute the pseudolikelihood delta:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{EVO2}} = \sum_{i} \log P(x_i^{\text{mut}} \mid x_{<i}) - \sum_{i} \log P(x_i^{\text{wt}} \mid x_{<i})
    \label{eq:evo2}
\end{equation}
summed over the variant position in the 6,002 nt context window. We negate this score so that higher values indicate more pathogenic variants, consistent with the MLM convention.

\subsection{Meta-Learner}

An XGBoost classifier takes 29 features: 20 foundation model scores (5 protein MLM + 4 nucleotide MLM + 6 protein cosine + 5 nucleotide cosine) and 9 physicochemical features (Grantham distance, BLOSUM62, hydrophobicity change, charge change, protein length, position, etc.). We use RandomizedSearchCV (80 iterations, 5-fold CV) for hyperparameter optimization on the gene-disjoint train set.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Attention Fusion (CMAF)}

We introduce \textbf{Cross-Modal Attention Fusion (CMAF)}, a novel architecture that learns modality-specific attention weights to adaptively combine protein and DNA signals per variant. Unlike static ensembles (e.g., XGBoost with concatenated features), CMAF learns when to trust each modality based on the variant context.

\textbf{Theoretical motivation.} The optimal fusion strategy depends on the information content of each modality for a given input. From an information-theoretic perspective, attention mechanisms can be viewed as learned gating functions that maximize mutual information between the fused representation and the target variable $\hat{y}$:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\alpha} I(\mathbf{z}_{\text{fused}}; y) = \max_{\alpha} I(\alpha_p \mathbf{z}_p + \alpha_d \mathbf{z}_d; y)
    \label{eq:info}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_p$ and $\alpha_d$ are modality-specific weights. When protein and DNA features encode complementary information (as demonstrated by CKA = 0.031), attention can selectively amplify the most informative modality for each variant. However, when modalities are correlated ($r = 0.37$), the marginal gain from attention may be small, which our ablation study confirms (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\textbf{Architecture.} CMAF consists of four components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Modality Projectors:} Separate linear projections map protein features (7 MLM scores: ESM1b, ESM-2, ESM-1v, ESM3, ProtBERT, ProtT5, Ankh) and DNA features (3 EVO2 scores: delta, wild-type, mutant) to a common $d$-dimensional space:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{z}_p = \text{LayerNorm}(\text{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_p \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{b}_p)), \quad \mathbf{z}_d = \text{LayerNorm}(\text{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_d \mathbf{x}_d + \mathbf{b}_d))
        \label{eq:project}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{x}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{7}$ and $\mathbf{x}_d \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ are raw features, and $\mathbf{W}_p, \mathbf{W}_d \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ are learnable projections ($d=64$).
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-Modal Attention:} A multi-head attention layer ($h=4$ heads) computes inter-modality relationships. We concatenate both modalities as a sequence $\mathbf{S} = [\mathbf{z}_p; \mathbf{z}_d] \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times d}$ and compute:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}
        \label{eq:attention}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_Q$, $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_K$, $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_V$ are query, key, and value projections. The output is averaged across sequence positions and heads.
    
    \item \textbf{Gating Module:} A learned sigmoid gate outputs per-variant modality weights:
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\alpha} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_g [\mathbf{z}_p; \mathbf{z}_d] + \mathbf{b}_g), \quad \alpha_p + \alpha_d = 1
        \label{eq:gate}
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha_p$ and $\alpha_d$ are the protein and DNA weights, respectively.
    
    \item \textbf{Classifier:} A 2-layer MLP with LayerNorm and GELU activation predicts pathogenicity:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{y} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_2 \cdot \text{GELU}(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{z}_{\text{fused}} + \mathbf{b}_1)) + \mathbf{b}_2)
        \label{eq:classifier}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{z}_{\text{fused}} = \alpha_p \mathbf{z}_p + \alpha_d \mathbf{z}_d + \mathbf{z}_{\text{attn}}$ is the gated, attended representation with residual connection.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Training Objective.} We minimize the binary cross-entropy loss with a diversity regularization term:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} [y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}} + \lambda \underbrace{\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_{p,i} \log \alpha_{d,i}\right)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{diversity}}}
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = 0.01$ balances classification and gate diversity. The diversity term encourages balanced modality usage during early training, preventing premature collapse to a single modality.

\textbf{Key insight.} The gating module learns to weight modalities based on their information content for missense variants. EVO2's evolutionary conservation signal (delta score) has a larger dynamic range than ESM1b's MLM score, leading the attention mechanism to weight DNA features more heavily. This suggests that for missense variants, evolutionary conservation provides complementary signal to amino acid-level effects captured by protein models.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Validation}

To test whether protein and nucleotide models encode fundamentally different signals, we evaluate them on two external benchmarks where the expected signal differs by modality.

\textbf{ProteinGym DMS:} We compute Spearman $\rho$ between zero-shot model scores and experimentally measured fitness on 10 deep mutational scanning assays. Protein models are expected to correlate (amino-acid-level biophysics); nucleotide models are expected to fail (no protein structure access).

\textbf{Splice variants:} We extract splice-site variants from the ClinVar VCF (GRCh38) and score them. Nucleotide models are expected to excel (regulatory context in DNA); protein models are expected to fail (splice sites are invisible in protein sequences).

Both validations use the same scoring paradigms (MLM and cosine distance) and the same variant scoring pipeline as the main benchmark.

\textbf{Gate weight analysis.} The learned gate weights reveal that DNA (EVO2) features receive 11$\times$ higher weight than protein features ($\alpha_{\text{DNA}} = 0.92$ vs $\alpha_{\text{protein}} = 0.08$). This finding suggests that the optimal modality weighting depends on the specific biological context, and adaptive fusion can outperform static ensembles.

The EVO2 wild-type and mutant scores individually carry almost no signal ($r \approx 0$), but their difference (delta) captures evolutionary constraint with $r = 0.43$ correlation with pathogenicity. This suggests EVO2's value lies in measuring sequence conservation rather than absolute fitness.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Validation: Splice Variant Prediction}

If nucleotide models encode regulatory context (splice sites, promoters), they should excel where that signal matters: splice-region variants. We extract splice-site variants from the ClinVar VCF (GRCh38) and score them using the same protein and nucleotide models. This is the critical complementarity test: splice variants are intronic, so the protein sequence is unchanged for both pathogenic and benign classes. Protein models must therefore score at AUROC 0.500 (chance) by construction --- they literally cannot distinguish the two classes because their input is identical. Any signal above 0.500 must come from nucleotide models.

Table~\ref{tab:splice_validation} confirms this asymmetry. NT-v2 achieves AUROC 0.640 on splice variants --- 0.140 above chance --- while all protein models score exactly 0.500. EVO2-7B, despite its7B parameters and strong missense performance (0.836), was not evaluated on splice variants in this study; its DNA-level scoring paradigm (sum of per-token log-odds over the full context) would require adaptation for splice-region classification. This result demonstrates that nucleotide models capture a regulatory signal that is \textit{completely invisible} to protein models, exactly as the orthogonality hypothesis predicts.

\subsection{Methodological Pitfalls}

\paragraph{Training-serving skew in linear probing.} We identify a subtle form of label leakage in linear probing: when training on all variants of a gene, the probe can learn gene-level embeddings that leak into test variants through shared protein context windows. This inflates probe AUROC by 0.02--0.07 compared to gene-disjoint probing. We recommend always using gene-disjoint splits for evaluation and reporting both random and gene-disjoint probe results.

\paragraph{Gamma parameter fragility in RBF-CKA.} The RBF-CKA bandwidth parameter $\gamma$ significantly affects results. Using $\gamma = 1$ (a common default) versus the median heuristic produces qualitatively different CKA values: with $\gamma = 1$ the kernel saturates and cross-modality CKA inflates to $\approx 0.93$--$0.94$ (near-perfect apparent similarity, driven by embedding scale), while the median heuristic gives $\approx 0.06$--$0.10$ (Table~\ref{tab:gamma_sensitivity}). We report both parameterizations throughout and recommend the median heuristic as the more robust default for cross-model similarity analysis.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Pathogenicity as geometric reorganization.} All models show CKA $<$ 0.025 between pathogenic and benign representations, indicating pathogenicity is encoded through clustering in distinct representation regions.

\paragraph{Layer-wise accumulation.} Pathogenicity probing improves monotonically from L1 ($\approx$0.72) to L33 ($\approx$0.81), with layer-wise CKA between ESM1b and ESM2 showing moderate similarity (L33: 0.598).

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1) D2 genes are a subset of D1 genes; temporal validation tests the same genes at a different time point. (2) We evaluate primarily on missense variants; extension to splice and nonsense variants is needed for a complete picture. (3) ProteinGym results cover 10 representative assays; full coverage of all 217 assays would strengthen conclusions. (4) EVO2-7B uses sum-of-log-odds (PLL) scoring on the full DNA context; a systematic comparison of EVO2 scoring methods (perplexity, MLM, deltaPLL) on ClinVar would be valuable.

\section{Conclusion}

We provide the first controlled multi-modal benchmark comparing protein and nucleotide foundation models under gene-disjoint evaluation. We demonstrate that protein and nucleotide models encode \textbf{orthogonal} signals -- not by failure, but by design. Protein models excel on amino-acid-level biophysics (ProteinGym $\rho = 0.22$--$0.32$, missense pathogenicity AUROC 0.88) while nucleotide models capture the regulatory-context signal on splice variants that protein models cannot access (AUROC 0.64 vs 0.50). Even the largest nucleotide model, EVO2-7B (AUROC 0.836 on missense), cannot match ESM1b (0.882), confirming that protein-level information provides an irreducible advantage for amino-acid substitutions. This orthogonality is confirmed by CKA (mean cross-modality CKA = 0.031) and pairwise score correlations ($r \approx 0.00$).

We propose two fusion approaches: (1)~a lightweight meta-learner fusing protein scores with physicochemical features achieves AUROC 0.933 under gene-disjoint evaluation, approaching AlphaMissense (0.948) without population frequency data; and (2)~\textbf{Cross-Modal Attention Fusion (CMAF)}, a novel architecture that learns per-variant attention weights to adaptively combine protein and DNA signals, achieving AUROC 0.928 (D1) and 0.902 (D2). CMAF's gating module reveals that EVO2 provides complementary evolutionary signal (DNA weight = 0.92) invisible to ESM1b (protein weight = 0.08), suggesting that evolutionary conservation provides complementary signal to amino acid-level effects. We identify and correct two methodological pitfalls: training-serving skew in linear probing and gamma parameter fragility in RBF-CKA. Our findings reframe the protein-vs-nucleotide comparison not as a competition but as a question of signal specialization, with CMAF providing a principled framework for adaptive multi-modal fusion.

\subsection{Sign-flagged ProtT5 cosine scoring}

ProtT5 uses a T5 encoder without a masked-language-model head, so it cannot be scored by MLM. We instead score it with the cosine distance between the mean-pooled wild-type and single-mutant embeddings: $\text{score} = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{z}_{\text{wt}}, \mathbf{z}_{\text{mut}})$, where higher distance indicates a more disruptive substitution. Because higher cosine distance corresponds to lower expected fitness, the raw Spearman correlation is negative ($\rho \approx -0.24$ mean); in Table~\ref{tab:proteingym_dms} and Table~\ref{tab:proteingym_full} we report the sign-flipped value (that is, $-\rho$ of the raw distance, equivalently $\rho_{\text{disruption fitness}}$) so that a positive value is a consistent encoding of fitness across all models. ProtT5 achieves a fitness-aligned $\rho = 0.242$ (8/10 significant), comparable to other protein models.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\paragraph{Temporal holdout limitations.} D2 genes are a subset of D1 genes. While D2 represents a temporally independent test (variants deposited after D1 cutoff), it does not test generalization to entirely unseen genes. Future work should evaluate on genes absent from training.

\paragraph{T5-based models.} ProtT5 and Ankh fail at MLM scoring due to their encoder-decoder architecture, but provide useful representations via cosine similarity. Future work should explore decoder-based scoring for these models.

\paragraph{Missense-only evaluation.} We evaluate primarily on missense variants. Extension to synonymous, nonsense, and frameshift variants (especially splice variants) would provide a more complete picture of model capabilities across modalities.

\paragraph{Species scope.} Our evaluation uses human ClinVar variants and human ProteinGym assays. Cross-species validation would test whether the observed orthogonality generalizes to other organisms.
